\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked what an LLM contributes when it writes and rewrites a grammar-based
generator from a formal grammar and black-box feedback alone, with no
coverage instrumentation. On cJSON, its generators raised acceptance from a
static baseline's 58.2\% to 97.1\% over fifteen seeded runs. A pre-registered
control showed where that gain comes from: with every campaign result
withheld, the LLM's generators were statistically indistinguishable
($p \approx 0.95$, $\delta = -0.02$). The model writes a valid generator from
the grammar; the counts the loop feeds back do not make it better, and the
one thing the feedback measurably helps with is avoiding proposals that fail
to run. A pre-registered replication on parson reversed the acceptance
effect, and every rejected input was explained: most contain constructs a
conformance suite says a parser must accept, so the generators reached
exactly where parson departs from the standard, and the acceptance score
counted it as failure. No crash was found on either parser.

The lesson for coverage-free fuzzing, which matters for targets
coverage-guided fuzzers cannot instrument -- pinned vendored dependencies,
closed binaries, embedded or cross-compiled parsers -- is that cheap
black-box counts are not enough to steer an LLM. Useful feedback has to
carry content: which inputs failed and why, and where implementations of
the same grammar differ. The same grammar admits directly opposite validity
decisions on two parsers (duplicate keys accepted by one, rejected by the
other), which is exactly the target-specific knowledge a formal
specification cannot supply and a refinement loop would need.

control arm's protocol, and the reproducibility tooling described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, so that every comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} can be checked, extended, or falsified
directly.\footnote{\url{\artifacturl}}
