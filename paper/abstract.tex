Grammar-based generators keep fuzzing inputs syntactically valid, but they
are written once and rarely revised. We ask what a large language model
(LLM) contributes when it writes and rewrites such a generator from a
formal grammar and black-box parser feedback alone, with no coverage
instrumentation. Our pipeline targets the cJSON parser: an LLM proposes
Hypothesis generators, every proposal is sandboxed and validated before it
runs, and a sanitizer-instrumented harness classifies each input as
accepted, rejected, or crashing. Over fifteen seeded runs, the LLM's
generators raise acceptance from 58.2\% for a static baseline, half of
whose inputs are malformed by design, to 97.1\%. A pre-registered control
locates the source of this gain. With every campaign result withheld from
the prompt, the LLM's generators are statistically indistinguishable
(92.9\%; $p \approx 0.95$, Cliff's $\delta = -0.02$): the gain comes from
the model writing a valid generator from the grammar, not from the
feedback, and a feedback ablation agrees. Feedback's one measurable effect,
in an exploratory analysis, is that fewer proposals fail to run (29 against
52 of 75). The static baseline's valid half alone accepts every input and
yields more distinct input shapes than either LLM arm. A pre-registered
replication on a second parser, parson, reverses the acceptance effect
(95.6\% to 92.4\%, $p \approx 0.024$): 614 of the 1{,}063 inputs parson
rejected contain constructs that the JSONTestSuite conformance suite says
a parser must accept, so the refined generators reached exactly where
parson departs from the standard, and the acceptance score counted this as
failure. No crash occurred in 25{,}500 sanitizer-instrumented executions.
Black-box counts are cheap to compute but tell an LLM too little to improve
a generator; useful feedback must say why inputs fail and which ones
matter. We release the pipeline, harnesses, and all campaign data.
